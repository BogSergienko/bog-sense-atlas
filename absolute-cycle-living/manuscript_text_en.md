00.4 / THE BOUNDARY
Where does the problem end?

NOT 'SCIENTISTS HID THE TRUTH,' BUT 'HOW LIMITS DEFINE A QUESTION'



Our hypothesis for the reader: scientific constraints both make a statement precise and can
confine inquiry to one chosen formulation. A theorem cannot be blamed for holding under
explicit assumptions. But we can — and should — ask what lies beyond those assumptions.

Mathematics repeatedly changes its language: geometry to algebra, shapes to flows, local
enumeration to global invariants. That does not imply all century-old problems share an
answer. It gives us a fertile method: name the constraint, alter one condition deliberately, form
a new problem, then return to the original.

The 19 knots in this book are not a list of our victories. They are tools for readers to observe
their own reasoning and test the same move against the problem of reconstructing time.





   THE QUESTION THAT CHANGES THE VIEW

   What exactly was proved impossible: solving the problem, or solving it with the previously
   allowed operations? That distinction leads from angle trisection to the history of nested
    cycles.

NOT A BATTLE FOR THE WORD 'DISCOVERY' — A METHOD FOR SEEING PAST A QUESTION'S EDGE

operations. That is what makes the theorem strong. But if we forget that researchers chose the
boundaries, we can mistake a method's limits for the limits of thought itself.

This is not an accusation against scientists or a claim of concealed facts. It invites future
researchers to draw the frame, step outside it, admit honestly that a new problem has been
formed, and investigate whether a bridge back exists.

Our bridge became preserved history. Without it, one phase or shared period may hide many
underlying systems. We did not 'solve the old theorems'; we developed a way to pose a
different question and learn from it.
